# Robodrama

About a decade ago during my time as a competitor, there was a term that a district-level robotics coordinator used to describe the type of student drama that occurred with students in robotics.&#x20;

_**"Robodrama"**_

Such a simple and elegant term, and a lot more under the surface. The same patterns repeat time and time again.&#x20;

But why? How can an educational engineering project produce this type of churn and burn?

#### Some Quick Examples

I've seen quite a few things in my time as both a mentor, project developer, and competitor in the VEX robotics community. This obviously isn't a comprehensive list but off the top of my head here are a couple of incidents that are easy to talk about:

1. Mutually Assured Destruction at States
   1. Team at the California State Championship level decides to call out another team for drilling holes in their pneumatics illegally after losing to them in the quarterfinals. Both teams on each side are well known teams and very popular.
   2. Causes heated argument, entire half bracket DQ'd for general dishonesty on both sides (1 for withholding information and losing, the other for breaking the rules). Other side of the bracket's quarterfinalist winner wins the state competition.
   3. Deciding not to name this person, but well known competitor from Ohio decides to spam death threats to the VEX RSM (Regional State Manager's) email in response. Police get involved, competitor is banned from competing.
2. Various Abuses of "Leadership"
   1. Defaulting to pulling rank immediately, or shutting down all other ideas that aren't the "leaders'" ideas.
   2. Piling all paperwork (notebooking/logging) onto one member instead of evenly distributing it.
   3. Forcing themselves to be the robot driver, despite potentially not being the best fit for the job.
   4. Shutting other students out of interviews or events because they didn't show up as much compared to the rest of the team.
3. Technical Disagreements Leading to Complete Team Splits
   1. A tale as old as time in the VEX robotics world, team members disagree on direction, 2 robots end up being built, often neither of which are that good.&#x20;
   2. The team either splits or decides on one of the designs but there's still interpersonal friction no matter which direction is chosen.
4. Drive Team Drama
   1. Multiple people on the team want to be drivers of the robot on the team since they get center stage and all the attention (and it's fun).
   2. Disagreement on who gets to be the driver leads to either tryouts or even worse, team members splitting matches and having everyone drive.
   3. Performance is subpar at competition since some if not all drivers don't have much drive practice experience nor time to do work.

#### Theater Students with STEM Interests

Traditionally, "theater kids" tend to be get a bad rap for being the quirky, outspoken, and generally dramatic kids.

It occurred to me somewhere along the way that there's a lot of common ground between robotics and theater kids:

* After school activity that can easily take many hours.
* **Attended by students who are somewhat traditionally seen as "outcast" or folks who don't seem to be "well adjusted"** (nerds?**,** self aware that probably I fit into this group).
  * Symptomatically manifesting as a lack of self awareness and communication.
  * Nothing wrong with this... just an observation. Students are still learning for a reason.
* High stress environment with impending deadlines and emotional investment.
* Interpersonal competition within the activity itself between team members.

While the bullet points above can be applied to _many_ if not most extracurriculars, I hypothesize that the 2nd bullet point above may be the key differentiator between why robotics tends to have quite a bit of drama. Not to mention the direct nature of robotics being a competition between different teams.\
\
The only difference these days, being that your average quirky theater kid now has a chance to do an extracurricular that may be net them a stable and well paying career in the future.

#### The Alternative: Small Private Teams

Due to factors listed above, an interesting trend (at least in VEX robotics), is the prominent high performance of private 1-2 person teams. I wish I had more data on this, but generally speaking if students had enough resources and drive some would start their own private teams, with a low amount of people (somewhere between 1-3 people).&#x20;

These teams normally do very well, and judges would be very impressed that one or two people could pull off that much work compared to the 4-8 people teams from public schools. The lack of red tape and differing opinions allows decisions to be made much quicker.

I have a (very hot take) that the 1-2 person teams mentioned earlier should be discouraged in VEX. While it's substantially more work to have less members, there's a core collaborative and project management element missing from the educational robotics experience when most of the team is removed.

#### Radical Thesis: Something to Point and Laugh At

So, is there any point in attempting to solve this problem? Is there any _reasonable_ way to prevent this sort of behavior?\
\
My take is yes, but it would take quite a bit of work to get working.

I assert that if there's a way to make an artificial "lolcow" team or individual exhibiting toxic traits in a believable but funny way, this would generally curve the sort of behavior that we see. Best if the entire thing is played straight as a real situation.

Why? This gives students something to laugh at. It organically creates a feeling of "Oh I'd never do that" with certain practices that lead to toxic team dynamics and drama. A good professional level analogue would be the show _Silicon Valley,_ with absurdities in the tech industry brought to light in humorous ways.

Producing such material however, is a whole subject on itself. What medium would you develop it on? How would you popularize such material? Is there a way to do it ethically without making fun of real and serious strife between students in bad faith?\

#### Final Thoughts

I don't think "robodrama" is limited to just educational robotics.

At a certain point, engineering challenges aren't always about the best technical solution, but rather navigating stakeholder conversations with the people skills necessary for something to be produced in a reasonable way.

Navigating around disagreement not as a thing to be avoided at all costs, but rather an outlet for making better decisions is something that needs to be emphasized more as a critical skill.&#x20;

Truthfully, the interpersonal drama at a workplace isn't too far from what we see at the school level. Adults (in most cases) are just better at containing disagreements and emotionally more mature. Not to mention, there's money and jobs on the line so sometimes it's easier to just sit back and take a paycheck.

Getting this type of professionalism instilled early on in robotics should be emphasized just as much as programming, building, or any other technical element of STEM education. Of course, that's easier said than done...
